Unit 12: Recent Developments and Review

Advanced Object-Oriented Design and Programming

Unit Summary

During this unit, I consolidated and applied the knowledge developed across Units 1–11 through the BankSecure Capstone Project. Rather than treating object-oriented design, design patterns, secure coding, concurrency, persistence, software architecture, testing and Dependency Injection as separate topics, the Capstone required them to work together within one integrated Python application.

Unit 12 also introduced additional final-assessment requirements. These included applying the required advanced design patterns within one system, demonstrating AI-ready architecture and testing AI-dependent components without relying on a live model or external API. The unit also introduced conceptual awareness of AI-oriented components such as Model Registry for model lifecycle and version management and Feature Store for reusable feature management (MLflow, no date; Feast, no date).

Reflection

Unit 12 changed the focus from learning individual techniques to understanding how they interact within one system. BankSecure showed that a design can be functionally correct while still requiring careful consideration of security, concurrency, persistence, rollback and failure handling.

The Capstone also made me more selective when applying design patterns. I became more concerned with the problem each pattern solves and whether the additional abstraction improves the design rather than simply whether the pattern can be implemented.

The new AI-oriented requirements also helped me distinguish between building a production AI system and designing software that is prepared to integrate AI components. BankSecure demonstrates the second approach through replaceable service abstractions rather than claiming to implement a production machine-learning model.

Overall, Unit 12 consolidated the technical learning from the module while extending it into system integration, AI-ready architecture and critical evaluation of the final design.

End of Module Assignment – Tasks 1–4

Task 1 - Summarise Key Learning Outcomes

Throughout the module, practical work and the BankSecure Capstone shifted my focus from isolated functions to responsibilities, abstractions and testable behaviour. This strengthened my ability to evaluate and apply maintainable, secure and extensible object-oriented design. (Bass, Clements and Kazman, 2021).

Secure and Robust Software Development

Units 7 and 10 developed my secure coding skills through password hashing, password policy, temporary lockout and tests covering successful and rejected operations. Unit 9 reinforced service layer authentication, while Unit 6 developed robustness through thread-safe banking. Consistent lock ordering reduced circular-wait deadlock risk, and bidirectional transfers preserved the combined balance (Coffman, Elphick and Shoshani, 1971). I later extended these principles in BankSecure through service layer authorisation and negative RBAC testing. These activities showed that security controls must be integrated into workflows and validated against failure conditions (OWASP Foundation, n.d.).

Advanced OOP and Design Patterns

Units 1 and 2 developed my ability to apply and evaluate polymorphism, responsibility separation and SOLID principles. Units 3–5 then applied Factory Method, Decorator and Strategy to separate object creation, extend behaviour through composition and support interchangeable behaviour. BankSecure extended this learning through Abstract Factory and Visitor. These exercises helped me evaluate how patterns support separation and extensibility, while Unit 8 reinforced that additional abstraction should only be introduced when justified by the problem (Gamma et al., 1994).

Architecture, Testing and AI-Readiness

Unit 9 developed my ability to analyse architectural responsibility boundaries by separating user interaction, business services and data access. Unit 11 linked Dependency Injection to testability through interchangeable notification services and mock collaborators (Fowler, 2004). Unit 10 allowed me to apply Red–Green–Refactor and evaluate how tests can guide implementation and protect behaviour during change (Beck, 2002).

I applied these principles in BankSecure through repository abstractions, externally assembled dependencies and SQLite transaction handling. The fraud-service interface also provides a boundary for future AI integration while remaining deterministic. My conceptual study covered model lifecycle and version management through a Model Registry (MLflow, n.d.) and reusable feature management through a Feature Store (Feast, n.d.).

Advanced Design Patterns

The four required patterns were used only where a clear design problem was addressed. For simpler requirements, such as the daily transfer limit, the logic was kept as a business policy rather than introducing another pattern (Gamma et al., 1994; Fowler, 2018).

Architecture, Persistence and Security

Business services depend on the BankRepository abstraction, while SQLiteBankRepository provides persistence. ApplicationContainer acts as the Composition Root, assembling repositories, policies, fraud services, auditing and authorization outside the business services. This reduces coupling and supports isolated mocking while retaining real database integration tests (Fowler, 2004; Martin, 2017).

BankSecure stores monetary values as integer cents and uses explicit SQLite transactions so failed financial operations can roll back before commit. Deterministic account-lock ordering reduces circular-wait deadlock risk (Coffman, Elphick and Shoshani, 1971), favouring consistency over maximum parallelism.

Authentication and secure development controls were also evaluated against OWASP and NIST guidance (OWASP Foundation, n.d.; Scarfone, Souppaya and Dodson, 2022).

Transfer Sequence and Concurrency Flow

Figure 1 UML sequence diagram for the BankSecure transfer workflow

The sequence shows how TransferService handles validation, deterministic account locking, fraud assessment and persistence. Locks are acquired in a consistent order to reduce deadlock risk. If the fraud check approves the transfer, the repository updates both balances and records the transfer within one database transaction. Database failures are rolled back, while fraud rejection stops the process before repository.transfer() is called. Outcomes are recorded through the audit layer, ensuring failed or rejected transfers do not leave partially updated balances.

Mocking and AI-dependent testing

Using unittest.mock, TransferService is tested with mocked repository, policy, fraud, explanation and notification dependencies. This permits deterministic AI-provider failure tests without a live model or API (Meszaros, 2007). For example, a simulated timeout must stop processing before persistence. AI assistance was also used to suggest candidate test cases and edge conditions and to support regression review, but expected behaviour and assertions were still checked through engineering judgement and manual validation.

Inspect README selected evidence: Comprehensive Code Evidence → AI Fraud Detection / AI-Readiness

Stress and Scalability Results

The stress test uses 20 threads with 100 transfers per thread and checks both error count and balance conservation. The scalability benchmark repeats the workload at 1, 5, 10, 20 and 40 threads, recording elapsed time, operations per second, errors and final balance. Exact performance varies by machine; the key correctness result is that errors remain zero and the combined balance remains conserved at every tested level.

Figure 3 Stress-test evidence: zero errors and conserved combined balance

Figure 4 Scalability-test evidence: zero errors and balance conservation from 1 to 40 threads

Figure 5 Scalability benchmark showing throughput as concurrency increases from 1 to 40 threads

The benchmark shows an overall reduction in throughput as concurrency increases from 1 to 40 threads. However, every test level completed with zero errors and preserved the expected combined balance.

The results show the trade-off in the current design: correctness was maintained as contention increased, while throughput gradually decreased.

Critical Commentary

The main design trade-off was between useful abstraction and unnecessary complexity. Strategy, Abstract Factory, Decorator and Visitor were kept because each solved a specific problem, while simpler requirements remained as services or business rules (Gamma et al., 1994; Bass, Clements and Kazman, 2021). Repository and Dependency Injection added extra interfaces, but reduced coupling to SQLite and improved test isolation (Fowler, 2004; Martin, 2017).

Testing required different techniques for different risks. Mocks covered isolated failure paths, while SQLite, integration and stress tests were used where real component interaction mattered (Meszaros, 2007). Deterministic locking reduced parallelism under contention, but protected balance consistency (Coffman, Elphick and Shoshani, 1971).

The project also has limits. According to SQLite (n.d.), only one writer can modify a database file at a time, which can limit write concurrency under heavier concurrent workloads. In-process locks also do not represent distributed transaction handling.

Task 3 - Reflect on Skill Development

Critical Thinking and Problem-Solving

My main development was moving from asking whether code worked to evaluating whether a solution was maintainable, testable, secure and proportionate. Design-pattern and concurrency work challenged me to compare alternatives, justify abstraction and reason about problems such as race conditions and deadlock (Gamma et al., 1994; Fowler, 2018; Coffman, Elphick and Shoshani, 1971).

Research, Coding and Independent Learning

When concepts were unclear, I learned to break problems into smaller questions, consult academic sources and documentation, test solutions and apply the findings to the wider project. This made my learning systematic and improved my ability to justify implementation decisions independently.

Testing and Engineering Judgement

Testing moved beyond checking successful output. I learned to use mocks for isolated failures and integration, concurrency and stress testing where real component interaction mattered (Meszaros, 2007). Red-Green-Refactor also showed how testing can guide change rather than being left until the end (Beck, 2002).

Ethical and AI-Aware Design

I developed a broader view of bias, explainability, testability and auditability. In BankSecure, keeping AI-related behaviour behind interchangeable service interfaces supported testing and future replacement without tightly coupling the transfer logic (Tabassi, 2023).

Communication, Collaboration, Resilience and Time Management

Peer discussions improved my ability to explain decisions, consider alternative viewpoints and revise my judgement when another argument was better supported. BankSecure’s 17 iterations strengthened my resilience, while dividing implementation, testing, documentation and evidence into manageable stages improved my time management.

Overall, the module strengthened my ability to analyse, justify, communicate and adapt as a computing professional.
